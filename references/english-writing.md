# English Writing Guide

Reference for Write, Rewrite and Review modes on English text. The patterns below are diagnostic hints, not bans. Change a pattern only when it harms clarity, flow, accuracy or fit for the reader. A word that is precise, standard in the field or required by a source stays.

## Register first

Settle the register before editing: audience, genre, and how much formality the reader expects. Without samples from the user, choose a neutral register that fits the document. Do not raise formality to sound serious, or lower it to sound friendly, unless the genre asks for it.

## Wordiness and redundancy

- Cut words that add nothing: "in order to" (when "to" works), "due to the fact that" (because), "at this point in time" (now), "completely eliminate", "future plans".
- Keep a longer form when it carries a distinction: "in order to" can prevent a misreading, and "basic fundamentals" is not always redundant in a teaching text.
- Remove doubled qualifiers ("very unique", "somewhat of a") and repeated claims that restate the previous sentence.
- Do not shorten the document when the task is rewrite. Tightening sentences is different from deleting content.

## Transitions

- "Moreover", "Furthermore", "Additionally" in consecutive sentences usually mark a list of unrelated points. Ask what the real relation is: cause, contrast, example, sequence. Name it or drop the connective.
- Openers like "In today's fast-paced world" or "It is important to note that" say nothing about the subject. Start with the subject.
- A plain "but", "so" or "because" is often enough. Keep a formal connective when the register or the logic calls for it.
- Do not replace one stock transition with another. Make the next sentence refer to something specific in the last one.

## Generic corporate phrasing

- "Leverage synergies", "best-in-class", "drive value", "robust solution": replace with what the thing does, for whom, and how much, when the source provides that. If the source gives no specifics, flag the gap rather than invent numbers.
- Keep an established term of art when readers in the field expect it ("stakeholder", "latency budget").

## Repetitive structure and rhythm

- Check for runs of sentences with the same shape (subject, verb, object, same length) or the same opening word. Combine, reorder or subordinate only when the run reads flat.
- No quotas: do not alternate long and short sentences by rule, count commas, or avoid lists of three. A series of three items is fine when there are three items.
- Read the passage aloud. If a sentence needs a second pass to parse, restructure it; if it reads smoothly, leave it.

## Nominalization and jargon

- "Make a decision" becomes "decide", "perform an analysis of" becomes "analyze", "give consideration to" becomes "consider", when the shorter verb keeps the meaning.
- Keep the noun form when the noun is the topic ("the decision was delayed") or the field uses a fixed phrase ("conduct a survey").
- Replace jargon the reader will not know; keep it when the audience does. Define a term once at first use if some readers may lack it.

## Voice

Active voice is usually clearer when the actor matters. Passive voice is legitimate when the actor is unknown, irrelevant or conventionally omitted, as in methods sections ("Samples were centrifuged at 4 °C"). Do not convert passives to meet a percentage.

## Contractions

Fine in informal writing and in professional email when the register allows ("we'll send the file today"). Avoid in formal academic prose unless the style guide permits them. Never add contractions just to sound natural, and never remove them from a text whose voice depends on them.

## Certainty and generalization

- Match the verb to the evidence: "suggests", "is associated with", "is consistent with" for correlational or preliminary findings; "shows" or "demonstrates" only when the design supports it.
- Do not turn association into cause, a hypothesis into a result, or one study into "research proves".
- Check broad words ("always", "all", "everyone", "never") against the source. Narrow them if the source does.
- When a claim looks wrong, flag it; do not silently correct it.

## Spelling variety

Follow the user's variety or the style guide (American or British: color/colour, analyze/analyse, program/programme). Keep one variety throughout and never mix them. Do not alter spelling inside quotations, titles, citations, or code.

## Protected technical meaning

Keep API names, identifiers, commands, file paths, units and numbers exactly as written. Do not translate or "improve" a term whose meaning is defined by a spec, even if it reads awkwardly. If a rewrite could change what a technical sentence claims, keep the original and note the concern.

## Examples (before and after)

1. Before: "Moreover, the team made a decision to postpone the release. Furthermore, the team conducted a review of the backlog."
   After: "The team decided to postpone the release and reviewed the backlog."
   Reason: stacked connectives and weak nominal verbs.
2. Before: "In today's fast-paced world, data security is of paramount importance."
   After: "Data security deserves priority."
   Reason: the opener says nothing; add a specific reason or audience only if the source supplies one.
3. Before: "The results prove that the program improves outcomes."
   After: "The results suggest that the program is associated with better outcomes."
   Reason: in Review, flag the mismatch; in Rewrite, soften only when the source shows the design is observational.
4. Before: "We leverage a robust solution to drive value."
   After: "[specify: what the tool does and the measured benefit]"
   Reason: the source gives no specifics; flag the gap instead of inventing numbers.
5. Before: "Hi Dana, we'll send the revised draft by Friday."
   After: unchanged.
   Reason: KEEP. Contractions fit a professional email; removing them would only stiffen it.
6. Before: "Participants were randomized to two arms, and outcomes were assessed at 12 weeks."
   After: unchanged.
   Reason: KEEP. Passive voice is standard in methods and the actor is irrelevant.
7. Before: "Call `getUserById()` to retrieve the record, and then check for null."
   After: unchanged.
   Reason: KEEP. API name and instruction are exact; rewording risks changing behavior.

## Review checklist

- Does each sentence earn its place, or does it restate the previous one?
- Do connectives express a real relation, and would the paragraph still flow without some of them?
- Are claims calibrated to the evidence, with no association written as cause?
- Is the register consistent, and do contractions and spelling variety follow the style guide or user preference?
- Are nominal phrases and jargon kept only where the reader benefits?
- Are passive constructions justified by an unknown, irrelevant or conventional actor?
- Do code, identifiers, numbers, quotations and citations match the source exactly?
- Does the edit preserve length, depth and the author's voice unless the user asked for a change?
